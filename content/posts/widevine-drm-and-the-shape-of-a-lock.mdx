I want to say upfront what this post is and isn't. This is research notes on how Widevine's licensing flow and content packaging actually work, written from analysing a real deployment. It is not a distribution tool, and the source that came out of this work stays private for exactly that reason — understanding a lock and publishing a key are different acts, and only one of them is defensible as research.

## Why DRM is a systems problem, not a crypto problem

The cryptography behind Widevine — AES-128 content keys, a license server issuing them under policy — is not the interesting part. It's well-documented, well-implemented, and not where the real engineering lives. The interesting part is everything *around* the crypto: how a license gets negotiated, what a client is trusted to enforce on its own, and where that trust boundary actually sits.

That boundary is the whole system in miniature:

1. The client requests a license, presenting a certificate that asserts its security level (software-only L3, or a hardware-backed L1).
2. The license server responds with an encrypted key, plus a policy — can this be persisted, does it expire, what output protection does it require.
3. Enforcement of that policy happens **client-side**, inside a CDM (Content Decryption Module) the server has to trust it's actually talking to.

Step three is the whole ballgame. A server-issued policy is only as strong as the client's willingness to obey it — which is why L1 hardware-backed key handling exists at all: to move the trust boundary somewhere the client itself can't reach.

## Repackaging: the unglamorous half of the pipeline

The DRM analysis was one half of the project. The other half — repackaging a protected stream into a clean adaptive-bitrate HLS ladder with multi-audio and subtitle tracks intact — turned out to be the part with more edge cases per hour of work.

A representative step in that pipeline, remuxing extracted segments with MP4Box while preserving track structure:

```bash
MP4Box -add video.m4v#trackID=1:name="Video" \
       -add audio_jpn.m4a#trackID=1:name="Japanese" \
       -add audio_eng.m4a#trackID=1:name="English" \
       -add subs_eng.srt:name="English" \
       -new output_packaged.mp4
```

The failure mode that ate the most time wasn't decryption — it was **timestamp drift** between independently-extracted audio and video segments after remuxing, which surfaces as audio sync that's perfect for the first ten minutes and steadily wrong after. The fix was forcing a shared presentation timestamp origin across every track before the mux step, not after:

```python
def normalize_pts(tracks: list[Track], epoch: int) -> None:
    for track in tracks:
        offset = track.first_pts - epoch
        track.shift_pts(-offset)
```

Small function, expensive lesson: `first_pts` on an extracted track is not guaranteed to be zero, and assuming it is will pass every test on a five-minute sample and fail on anything long enough for drift to become audible.

## What actually generalises

Widevine is one DRM scheme, but the pattern — a trust boundary the server can't directly verify, enforced by a client under partial adversarial control — shows up everywhere: app attestation, hardware-backed key storage on mobile, even TLS client certificates in a zero-trust mesh. Understanding where that boundary actually sits, and what happens when you're on the wrong side of it, was the transferable part of this research.
